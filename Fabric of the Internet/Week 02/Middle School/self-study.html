Lesson 1

How Your Browser Finds a Website

You type youtube.com and the page shows up in a heartbeat. But computers don't actually talk to each other using names like "youtube.com" — they use numbers called IP addresses. So how does a friendly name turn into the exact machine that has your video? Two clever systems work together.

DNS — the Internet's phone book

  • DNS (the Domain Name System) turns a name like youtube.com into its IP number — the address of the computer that holds the site.
  • Just like a phone book turns a person's name into their phone number, DNS turns a website's name into the number your computer actually dials.

HTTP — the request-and-deliver rules

  • HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) is the set of rules your browser and the server use to ask for a page and hand it back.
  • Your browser says "please send me this page," and the server replies with the words, pictures, and video that build the page you see.

Type a name → ask DNS → get the number → HTTP fetches the page → it loads. That whole trip happens in a fraction of a second, every single time you open a website.

Lesson 2

Who Gets Access?

DNS and HTTP can find any website in the world — but only if you can get onto the Internet in the first place. And here's the thing: not everyone can, and not everyone gets there equally. Two ideas help explain why.

The Digital Divide — the access gap

  • The digital divide is the gap between people who have good, reliable Internet and the people who don't.
  • It can come from cost, where you live, or the devices you can afford. A student with fast home WiFi and a laptop has a very different experience than one who shares a phone or waits for the library to open.

Net Neutrality — treat all sites equally

  • Net neutrality is the idea that Internet providers should treat all websites equally — not speed up some and slow down others.
  • Without it, a company could make its own videos load fast while a small creator's site crawls, changing what you actually get to see and use.
Check your thinking

Question: A student has no home Internet but has a library nearby with computers that close at 6pm. Name TWO ways this affects their schoolwork.

Impact 1: No research at night

They can't do research-heavy homework in the evening, because the only computers they can use are locked up after 6pm.

Impact 2: Late or missed submissions

Assignments due online at night can't be submitted on time, and there's limited time to type long assignments before the library closes.

Also fair

No evening video calls with teachers for extra help, and less time to practice online tools everyone else uses at home.

The big idea: access isn't just "having Internet or not" — it's also when, where, and how much you get to use it.
