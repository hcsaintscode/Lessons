Finding Websites & Who Gets Access

Week 2 — DNS, HTTP, the digital divide, and an advocacy campaign

At a Glance

Driving Question
How does your browser find a website — and does everyone get the same access to the Internet?
Big Idea
DNS translates website names into addresses and HTTP fetches the page; but access to the Internet isn't equal — the digital divide affects real people, and we can advocate to close it.
Schedule
Day 1 Learn (45 min) · Day 2 Explore (90 min) · Day 3 Create (90 min)
Week Deliverable
An Internet Access Advocacy Campaign (poster or slides) on a student-chosen issue, presented to the class

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • Explain how DNS translates a website name into an IP address (the Internet's phone book)
  • Describe how an HTTP request/response delivers a web page to your browser
  • Interpret real data about the digital divide and explain net neutrality in plain terms
  • Design and present an advocacy campaign proposing a solution to an access problem they care about

Day 1 — Learn: How Your Browser Finds a Website

DNS, the Internet's phone book — and HTTP, the delivery

Time Activity
0–7 min Hook — "You type a name, not a number. How does that work?"
7–22 min Mini-lesson — DNS (name → IP) + HTTP (request → response)
22–36 min Classroom DNS simulation (unplugged)
36–42 min Trace it on your OWN device (personalized, optional live demo)
42–45 min Exit ticket

Hook (7 min)

"When you want a website, you type a NAME like 'youtube.com' — not a number. But computers find each other using IP addresses (numbers), which we learned last week. So how does 'youtube.com' turn into the right number? Who's the translator?"

Let them guess. The answer — DNS, the Domain Name System — is like a giant phone book that looks up the number for a name.

Mini-lesson: DNS + HTTP (15 min)

  • DNS — Domain Name System — translates human-friendly website names (like www.example.com) into numerical IP addresses. The Internet's phone book.
  • HTTP — HyperText Transfer Protocol — the rules your browser and a server use to request and deliver a web page.

"Let's walk the full trip. You type a name → your device asks DNS for the IP → DNS answers with the number → your browser sends an HTTP request to that server → the server sends back the page (an HTTP response). That whole round trip happens in a fraction of a second."

Analogy — DNS is looking up a friend's phone number by their name; HTTP is the actual phone call where you ask for something and they hand it over.

Classroom DNS Simulation (14 min)

"Let's BE the DNS system. Half of you are 'websites' with a name and a secret 'IP number.' The other half are browsers who must find a site's number by asking the class 'DNS' (a few students acting as the phone book). Walk around, look up a name, get its number, then 'connect.'"

  • Give ~6 students name cards ("catvideos.com", etc.) each with a made-up IP number.
  • Appoint 2–3 students as the DNS "phone book" holding the name→number list.
  • Everyone else is a browser: pick a site name, go to DNS, get the number, then find that student.
  • Debrief: "What if DNS was slow or down? What if it gave you the WRONG number?"

Make It Yours: Trace It on Your Own Device (6 min)

Optional live demo — on a class computer, show that a real site name maps to a real number. (Teachers: you can run nslookup youtube.com in a terminal, or just show that different names lead to different servers.) Keep it light; the point is that DNS is real.

"In your own words, what does DNS do, and what does HTTP do? Use the phone-book / phone-call analogy or your own."

Every student can explain that DNS turns a name into an IP address and HTTP fetches the page.

Day 2 — Explore: Who Gets Access?

The digital divide, net neutrality, and real people

Time Activity
0–5 min Warm-up — "What would you NOT be able to do without the Internet?"
5–20 min Mini-lesson — the digital divide + net neutrality
20–45 min Data dive — read real Pew Research data
45–70 min Persona case study (Diego, Lisa, Mr. Johnson, Aisha)
70–85 min Pick YOUR issue (personalized brainstorm for the campaign)
85–90 min Wrap + record vocab

Warm-up (5 min)

"Make a quick list: what would you NOT be able to do this week without the Internet — for school, fun, or family? Share a few. Now imagine not having reliable Internet at all. Millions of people don't."

Mini-lesson: The Digital Divide + Net Neutrality (15 min)

  • DIGITAL DIVIDE — the gap between people who have good access to the Internet and technology and those who don't.
  • NET NEUTRALITY — the idea that Internet providers should treat all websites and services equally, without slowing down or blocking some or charging for faster access.

"Let's talk about why these matter. When Internet access is unequal, it affects school, jobs, healthcare, and just staying connected to family and friends. And net neutrality decides whether every site loads fairly — or whether the ones who pay more get the fast lane."

Data Dive: Real Access Data (25 min)

"Let's look at REAL data on who has access. On your worksheet, record what you notice: who has the most access, who has the least, and what surprised you."

Pew Research · Internet & Broadband Fact Sheet

Focus on 2–3 comparisons (by income, by community type — urban/rural, by age). Keep it concrete: "What's the gap between the highest and lowest group?"

Persona Case Study (25 min)

"Meet four people dealing with real access challenges. With a partner, pick one and dig into their situation."

  • DIEGO — a high school student with no home Internet.
  • LISA — a small business owner in a rural area with unreliable Internet.
  • MR. JOHNSON — a retired senior who struggles with digital literacy and access.
  • AISHA — a college student affected by Internet speed restrictions.
  • For their chosen persona, students answer: What challenge does this person face?
  • How does it affect their life (school / work / health)?
  • What's one solution that could help?

Make It Yours: Pick Your Issue (15 min)

"Tomorrow you'll build an advocacy campaign. Today, choose the access issue YOU most want to help solve — it can be one of the personas, something in your own community or family, or an issue you've seen. Write down who it affects and why you care."

Encourage students to pick something personally meaningful — a relative without good Internet, their own neighborhood, a school issue. Ownership drives better campaigns.

Wrap + Vocab (5 min)

"Record today's vocabulary in your Unit Guide: DNS, HTTP, digital divide, net neutrality."

Every student interpreted real access data, analyzed a persona, and chose an issue for their campaign.

Day 3 — Create: Advocacy Campaign

Make a real case to close the digital divide

Time Activity
0–5 min Launch the campaign
5–25 min Research & define your message
25–55 min Build the campaign (poster or slides)
55–75 min Peer feedback
75–88 min Present / gallery walk
88–90 min Reflection

Launch the Campaign (5 min)

"Today you build an advocacy campaign about the access issue you chose — a real, sharable message that could actually persuade someone (a principal, a city, a company) to help close the digital divide."

Research & Define Your Message (20 min)

"On your worksheet, define: the problem, who it affects, one piece of evidence (from the Pew data or your persona), your proposed solution, and your call to action — what you want people to DO."

Build the Campaign (30 min)

"Make your campaign as a poster or a few slides. It needs: a clear title, the problem, who's affected, your evidence, your solution, and a call to action. Make it persuasive and make it yours — use your own voice."

Offer format choice — poster, slide deck, a short script for a video, or a social-post mockup. Choice increases ownership.

Peer Feedback (20 min)

"Trade with another team. Use the feedback form: What's the most compelling part? One question you have? One suggestion to make it stronger?"

Present / Gallery Walk (13 min)

"Gallery walk or short presentations. Each team shares their campaign and their call to action."

Extend Beyond the Classroom: Encourage students to actually SHARE their campaign — post it (with permission), show a family member, or send it to a relevant decision-maker. A campaign that leaves the classroom is real.

Reflection + Deliverable (2 min)

"Reflection: Why does the issue you chose matter to you? What's one thing you could personally do about Internet access in your own life or community?"

Week 2 Deliverable Rubric

For the advocacy campaign + reflection. Use it to give quick, consistent feedback.

Criteria Beginning Proficient Exemplary
Clearly states a real access problem and who it affects Problem is vague or unclear States the problem and who it affects Sharp, specific problem with a real audience
Uses evidence (data or persona) to support the case Little or no evidence Uses one piece of evidence Evidence is strong and clearly connected
Proposes a specific solution + call to action Solution is missing or unclear Names a solution and an action Specific solution with a clear, doable ask
Campaign is persuasive and reflects the student's own voice/choice Generic; little personal voice Persuasive with some personal voice Compelling; clearly the student's own choice

Every team presented an advocacy campaign with a problem, evidence, solution, and call to action.

Additional Notes for the Teacher

Differentiation

  • Struggling: Provide a campaign template with sentence frames ("The problem is ___. It affects ___. I want ___ to ___."); let them focus on one persona; a one-page poster is fine
  • Advanced: Have them find additional real data, address a counter-argument, or compare two possible solutions; propose a specific policy

Materials Needed

  • Poster paper / markers or a slide tool
  • The student worksheet
  • Projector
  • Computer access for the Pew data (or printed data snapshots)

Extend Beyond the Classroom

  • Students share their campaign with a family member or a decision-maker
  • Look for a real digital-divide moment in their own community

Optional: Code.org resources

  • AI Foundations Lessons 6–8 cover DNS/HTTP and the advocacy project with the Internet Simulator; optional — the core lessons need no Code.org login. studio.code.org/courses/ai-foundations-year1-2026

If you're running behind

  • The persona case study is the priority on Day 2; the DNS simulation can be shortened to a quick demo
  • Campaigns can be finished as homework; protect time for presentations
