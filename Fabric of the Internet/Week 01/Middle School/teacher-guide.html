How the Internet Moves Data

Week 1 — Infrastructure, bits, addresses, routing, and packets

At a Glance

Driving Question
How does the Internet move data from one device to another across the world?
Big Idea
The Internet is a global network of routers, switches, and servers that breaks data into bits and packets, addresses them, and routes them — with redundancy so it stays reliable.
Schedule
Day 1 Learn (45 min) · Day 2 Explore (90 min) · Day 3 Create (90 min)
Week Deliverable
A labeled map of the student's OWN network (home or school) + completed Unit Guide vocabulary

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • Name the core infrastructure of the Internet (router, switch, server, Internet tower) and describe what each does
  • Explain how data is represented as bits (binary) and why binary is reliable
  • Explain why devices need IP addresses and how routers move data between them
  • Describe how redundancy and packets make delivery reliable on an unreliable network

Day 1 — Learn: What Is the Internet, Really?

The physical stuff that connects the world

Time Activity
0–8 min Hook — "How would you connect two devices across the world?"
8–24 min Mini-lesson — infrastructure vocabulary + how a request travels
24–36 min Map YOUR OWN connection (personalized activity)
36–42 min Optional enrichment note (Internet Simulator) + share-out
42–45 min Exit ticket

Hook (8 min)

"Take out your phone — or picture it. When you send a message to someone across the world, that message physically travels there in a fraction of a second. How? What has to exist in the world for that to happen? Brainstorm every piece of equipment or connection you can think of."

Accept everything — WiFi, cell towers, satellites, cables. The goal is to surface that there's PHYSICAL infrastructure behind the magic. Then name the key pieces.

Mini-lesson: Infrastructure Vocabulary (16 min)

"The Internet can feel like magic, but it's built from real machines you could actually touch. Let's name the four key pieces and give each one a plain-language job."

  • ROUTER — a computer that forwards data across networks (like a mail-sorting hub that decides where a package goes next).
  • SWITCH — sends data to the right place inside a LOCAL network (like a receptionist directing you to the right room in one building).
  • SERVER — a powerful computer that stores and delivers websites, files, and messages when requested (like a library that hands you the book you ask for).
  • INTERNET TOWER — infrastructure that sends and receives wireless signals.

"Let's follow a request. You tap a link on your phone. That request leaves your device, goes to your router, travels across the Internet through many more routers, reaches a server that holds the website, and the server sends the page back the same way — all in under a second."

Emphasize the Internet is not "the cloud" — it's real cables, routers, and towers you could touch. "The cloud" is just someone else's servers in a building somewhere.

Make It Yours: Map Your Connection (12 min)

"Right now, YOUR device is connected to the Internet through real equipment. On your worksheet, sketch how your phone or laptop at home or school actually connects: your device → your WiFi router → your Internet provider → the wider Internet. Label the parts you just learned."

This personalizes the abstract vocabulary — every student's map is a little different (some have WiFi, some hotspot off a phone, some share one router for a whole family). Invite students to note something specific about THEIR setup.

Optional Enrichment + Share-Out (6 min)

If your class has Code.org accounts, the Internet Simulator (Lesson 1) lets pairs send messages and watch them travel. It's optional — this unit works fully without it.

Code.org · AI Foundations Year 1 (Internet Simulator)

"In your own words: what's the difference between a router, a switch, and a server? Give one real example of each from your own life if you can."

Every student can name router/switch/server and has sketched their own connection.

Day 2 — Explore: Bits, Addresses, and Getting There

Acting out how data actually travels

Time Activity
0–5 min Warm-up — video "The Internet: Wires, Cables & WiFi"
5–10 min Discuss binary
10–45 min Unplugged — Human Network + Binary Transmission
45–55 min Debrief bits & TCP
55–80 min IP addresses — why devices need them + routing-table read
80–90 min Reflect + record vocab

Warm-up: Video (5 min)

"Watch this short video on how the Internet physically sends data. As you watch, notice how computers turn information into simple signals."

Video · The Internet: Wires, Cables & WiFi (stop ~2:53)

Discuss Binary (5 min)

"Why is binary — just 1s and 0s — such a reliable way to send information? Turn and talk with a partner, then we'll share."

The insight: binary maps to simple physical states (on/off, light/dark) that are easy to detect even with a weak or noisy signal. Two states are hard to confuse; a hundred shades would be error-prone.

Unplugged: Human Network + Binary Transmission (35 min)

"We're going to BECOME a network. In groups, you'll connect to each other with string (each string = a direct connection), then send a message in binary using hand signals — and discover why sending data reliably is harder than it looks."

  • Groups of 5–7. Give string: a group of 5 needs 10 strings (4+3+2+1), a group of 6 needs 15, a group of 7 needs 21 (sum of all numbers below the group size) to fully connect everyone to everyone.
  • Have students physically connect every person to every other person with string — a "fully connected network."
  • Prompt: "Is this efficient? What happens as the group grows?" (This is the aha: full connection doesn't scale — real networks share routes.)
  • Assign roles (Sender, Receiver, and Network Monitors). The Sender transmits a short message like "HEY" in binary using hand signals (e.g., fist = 0, open hand = 1); the Receiver writes down the bits and decodes.
  • Network Monitors watch for dropped, flipped, or delayed bits.

Bits WILL get dropped or flipped — that's the point. Don't fix it for them; let them feel the unreliability.

Debrief: Bits & TCP (10 min)

"What went wrong when you sent the message? Real networks have the same problems — delays, congestion, errors. They solve it with PROTOCOLS. One key protocol is TCP."

  • TCP — Transmission Control Protocol — makes sure all parts of a message arrive and are put back in the right order.

IP Addresses (25 min)

"For a message to get to the right device, that device needs an ADDRESS — an IP address, like a home address for a computer."

  • IP ADDRESS — a unique number that identifies a device on a network, so data knows where to go.

"On your worksheet, use the routing table — device → IP → next-hop router — to trace which path the data takes to reach its destination."

Analogy — an IP address is like your mailing address; the router is the post office deciding which road the letter takes next.

Reflect + Vocab (10 min)

"Record today's vocabulary in your Unit Guide: bit, binary, TCP, IP address."

Every group transmitted a binary message and can explain why networks need protocols + addresses.

Day 3 — Create: Reliable Delivery: Routers, Redundancy & Packets

Designing a network that survives failure

Time Activity
0–5 min Recap
5–25 min Routers & redundancy — what happens when a router fails?
25–45 min Packets — breaking a message into pieces (unplugged packet sim)
45–65 min Design challenge — build a redundant network (personalized)
65–80 min TCP vs UDP tradeoff discussion
80–90 min Finish the "map your network" deliverable + reflection

Recap (5 min)

"Quick recall: what does a router do? A switch? A server? What's a bit? What's an IP address for?"

Routers & Redundancy (20 min)

"The Internet is huge and things fail all the time — cables get cut, routers go down. Yet it keeps working. Why? REDUNDANCY: there's more than one path between any two points."

  • REDUNDANCY — having more than one path or backup, so the network keeps working even when a part fails.

Analogy — if one road is closed, you take another route to the same place. The Internet was DESIGNED this way.

Packets (20 min)

"Big messages aren't sent all at once. They're broken into small PACKETS, each sent separately — maybe by different routes — and reassembled at the end."

  • PACKET — a small piece of a larger message, sent separately and reassembled at the destination.

"Let's try it. Write a sentence on your worksheet. Cut/number it into 4 'packets.' Trade with a partner but hand them the packets OUT of order and 'lose' one. Can they reassemble it? What would they need to ask for?"

This shows why packets are numbered (to reorder) and why TCP asks for missing packets to be resent.

Make It Yours: Design a Reliable Network (20 min)

"Now design a network for a place YOU care about — your home, your classroom, your dream gaming setup, your family's houses in different cities. Draw the devices and routers. Then make it REDUNDANT: add paths so that if any one router fails, everything stays connected. Mark the backup routes."

Let students pick a context that matters to them. Circulate and ask "what fails, and what's the backup path?"

TCP vs UDP (15 min)

"Two ways to send packets: TCP guarantees everything arrives in order (email, web pages) but is a bit slower. UDP is fast but doesn't guarantee delivery (live video, gaming) — a dropped frame is better than a laggy one."

  • UDP — User Datagram Protocol — sends packets fast without guaranteeing delivery or order; good when speed matters more than perfection.

"Discuss: for a video call vs. downloading a file — which would you use, and why?"

Deliverable + Reflection (10 min)

"Finish your network map — either your real connection or your designed redundant network. Then answer the reflection: Now that you know how the Internet moves data, what's one thing you understand differently about something you use every day?"

Additional Notes for the Teacher

Differentiation

  • Struggling: Provide a partly-labeled network diagram to complete; sentence starters for the reflection; run the binary activity with a 3-bit message
  • Advanced: Have them research and diagram how their data actually reaches a specific favorite site/app; add a second point of failure to the redundancy challenge; explore what a real traceroute shows

Materials Needed

  • Ball of string / yarn (for the Human Network)
  • Scissors
  • Index cards or paper for binary signals & packets
  • The student worksheet
  • Projector for the video

Extend Beyond the Classroom

  • At home, have students find their actual WiFi router and count how many devices connect to it
  • Ask a family member what their Internet provider is and how the house connects
