What Is a Computer?

Week 2 — Input, Output, Storage, Processing — and designing an app that solves a problem

At a Glance

Driving Question
How can the Input–Output–Storage–Processing model help us design an app that solves a problem?
Big Idea
Every computer works by taking Input, doing Processing, using Storage, and giving Output (IOSP). You can use IOSP plus the problem-solving process to design a solution.
Schedule
Day 1 Learn (45 min) · Day 2 Explore (90 min) · Day 3 Create (90 min)
Week Deliverable
Completed App Design Project Guide + an app poster, presented to the class

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • Define input, output, storage, and processing, and identify each in real devices
  • Explain the four kinds of processing (If/Then, Comparing, Finding a Match, Counting)
  • Apply the IOSP model to design an app that solves a real problem
  • Present and give feedback on an app design

Day 1 — Learn: What Is a Computer?

The four things every computer does

Time Activity
0–6 min Hook — "What made computers different from earlier machines?"
6–22 min Mini-lesson — IOSP vocabulary + the two Code.org videos
22–34 min Four kinds of processing
34–42 min Smartphone IOSP breakdown (think-pair-share)
42–45 min Exit ticket

Hook (6 min)

"Computers are everywhere — but what actually makes something a computer? What made the first computers different from machines that came before them, like a typewriter or a calculator?"

Take a few guesses. Don't correct yet — the payoff is that a computer can be REPROGRAMMED to do many different jobs, and it does it through Input, Processing, Storage, and Output.

Mini-lesson: IOSP Vocabulary (16 min)

"A computer isn't defined by having a screen or a keyboard. It's defined by four things it does with information. Let's name them: Input, Output, Processing, and Storage — IOSP. Learn these four words and you can understand any computer, from a phone to a game console to an ATM."

  • COMPUTER — a machine that works with information.
  • INPUT — information a computer gets (from users, devices, or other computers).
  • OUTPUT — information a computer gives (to users, devices, or other computers).
  • PROCESSING — the "thinking work" a computer does to turn input into output.
  • STORAGE — saving information to use in the future.

Show the two Code.org videos:

  • How Computers Work: Hardware & Software
  • How Computers Work: What Makes a Computer, a Computer?

Play one video (Hardware & Software) on Day 1; the second can be Day 2 warm-up if short on time.

Four Kinds of Processing (12 min)

  • IF/THEN — If something is true, do something else.
  • COMPARING — Check whether things are the same, or one is bigger.
  • FINDING A MATCH — Search a list for a match.
  • COUNTING — Keep track of how many.

"All the 'thinking' a computer does is built from simple operations like these four."

Smartphone IOSP Breakdown (8 min)

"Think-pair-share: For a smartphone, name one example each of Input, Output, Storage, and Processing."

Examples if pairs get stuck — Input: touchscreen, mic, camera; Output: screen, speaker; Storage: photos, apps; Processing: unlocking with your face.

"Give one real example each of Input, Output, Storage, and Processing from a device you use."

Every student can define IOSP and give a real example of each.

Day 2 — Explore: IOSP in the Wild + Launch the Project

Analyzing real devices, then starting your app design

Time Activity
0–5 min Review IOSP (optionally 2nd video)
5–30 min IOSP labeling lab — analyze real apps/devices in teams
30–40 min Debrief the lab
40–50 min Launch the App Design Project + find partners
50–75 min DEFINE the problem (partner work — from the project guide)
75–90 min Begin brainstorming the app (Prepare)

Review IOSP (5 min)

"Quick recall: what are the four IOSP words? And what are the four kinds of processing — If/Then, Comparing, Finding a Match, Counting?"

IOSP Labeling Lab (25 min)

"In teams, pick 3 everyday things — a video game, a smart speaker, an ATM, a fitness tracker, whatever — and break each one down into its Input, Output, Storage, and Processing. Fill in the table on your worksheet."

  • Push for specifics ("the screen" is output; "which button does what" is processing)
  • Ask "what does it STORE between uses?" — storage is the one students forget
  • Have teams share one surprising example

Launch the App Design Project (10 min)

"Now you become the designer. Over the rest of this week you'll design your OWN app that solves a real problem — using the problem-solving process from last week AND the IOSP model. You'll work with a partner and present a poster of your app."

Explain the deliverables (from the Lesson 8 project):

  • Work with a partner
  • Define a real problem
  • Brainstorm how an app could help
  • Identify the app's IOSP
  • Give and get peer feedback
  • Make a poster
  • Present

DEFINE the Problem (25 min)

"With your partner, first DEFINE a real problem worth solving. On your worksheet answer: What is the problem? Who does it affect? How will you know if it's solved? Use the criteria: interesting, well-defined, and computing is relevant."

Steer students toward "information problems" — things an app could actually help with (reminders, finding things, organizing, connecting people) rather than physical problems.

Begin Brainstorming (Prepare) (15 min)

"Name your app and describe what it does in a sentence or two — like you're telling a friend why they'd want it."

Every pair has defined a real problem and named their app.

Day 3 — Create: Design, Feedback, Present

Finishing the IOSP design and presenting the app poster

Time Activity
0–5 min Recap the project
5–35 min IOSP design — sketch the app + label inputs/outputs/storage/processing
35–50 min Peer review (swap with another pair)
50–70 min Make the poster
70–90 min Gallery walk / presentations

Recap the Project (5 min)

"Today you finish designing your app using IOSP, get feedback, make your poster, and present."

IOSP Design (30 min)

"Sketch a screen of your app. Then label it: What are the INPUTS (what does the app need from the user or sensors)? What are the OUTPUTS (what does it show or produce)? What does it STORE? What PROCESSING does it do to turn input into output? This is all on your worksheet."

Every app is just IOSP wearing a costume. If a pair is stuck, walk them through their app one action at a time: "The user taps this — that's input. Then the app does what? That's processing. Then it shows what? Output."

Peer Review (15 min)

"Swap projects with another pair. Use the peer review on your worksheet: name one thing that's strong, and one idea to improve. Look for gaps — did they forget storage? Is the processing clear?"

Make the Poster (20 min)

"Make a poster with: your app's name, the target audience, the problem it solves, the inputs it uses, a drawing of the output, and how it processes and stores information."

Present (20 min)

"Gallery walk or quick presentations. When you present, be ready to explain: the problem you defined, how your app works, its inputs and outputs, how it stores/processes info, and one change you made from peer feedback."

Week 2 Deliverable Rubric

For the App Design Project + poster. Use it to give quick, consistent feedback.

Criteria Beginning Proficient Exemplary
Defines a real, well-scoped problem Vague or off-topic problem Real problem, clearly stated Real, focused problem with audience + success measure
Identifies the app's Input, Output, Storage, Processing Names 1–2 of the four Names all four correctly All four, specific and well matched to the app
Poster clearly communicates the app design Missing key parts Includes required parts Clear, complete, easy to follow
Presentation explains the design + a change from feedback Unclear; no feedback change Explains design; names a change Clear explanation + thoughtful change from feedback

Every pair presented an app poster with a complete IOSP breakdown.

Additional Notes for the Teacher

Differentiation

  • Struggling: Provide an IOSP graphic organizer with sentence starters; let them design a simple app (a reminder or counter app) so IOSP is easy to map; give an example app fully worked
  • Advanced: Have them add a "four kinds of processing" analysis (which of If/Then, Comparing, Finding a Match, Counting does their app use, and where?); ask how the app would need to change to serve 1,000 users

Materials Needed

  • Student worksheet
  • Poster paper/markers (or a slide tool)
  • Projector for the videos
  • Devices for the IOSP labeling lab (optional)

Connecting to the Semester Arc

  • IOSP is the mental model for everything ahead. Next phase (Programming) students will BUILD apps; the inputs/outputs they designed here become real code. (High schoolers build a working prototype this week; middle schoolers design the poster and will build later.)

If you're running behind

  • The IOSP labeling lab can be shortened to 2 devices; protect time for the app design + presentation
  • Poster can be finished as homework; presentations are the priority
