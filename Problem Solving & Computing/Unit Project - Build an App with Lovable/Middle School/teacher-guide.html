Build an App with Lovable

Apply the problem-solving process and IOSP to design, build, and share a real app

At a Glance

Driving Question
Can you design and build a real app that solves a problem YOU care about?
Big Idea
You already know the problem-solving process (Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect) and the IOSP model. Now you'll use them to write a clear plan — and turn that plan into a working, shareable app with an AI builder.
Format
Project — flexible over ~3–5 sessions (see the phase tabs). Individual, pairs, or teacher-driven build.
Deliverable
A working app deployed to a shareable URL + a completed Project Guide + a showcase presentation

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • Apply DPTR to define a real problem worth solving
  • Translate an app idea into an IOSP spec (Inputs, Outputs, Storage, Processing)
  • Turn that spec into a clear natural-language prompt for an AI app builder
  • Build, test, and iterate a working app; then share and reflect on it

Setup — Before You Start

Accounts, credit budget, and expectations

"You've learned how to think through problems and how computers work. Now you become a builder: you'll make a REAL app that solves a problem you actually care about — and put it on the internet to share."

What Is lovable.dev?

  • AI APP BUILDER — lovable.dev is a tool where you describe the app you want in plain language, and it builds a working web app you can preview, change, and publish to a real web link.
  • NO CODE TYPING — you don't write code. In Week 2 you saw raw code in StackBlitz; here, lovable is your first real "build." The skill is DESCRIBING the app precisely and ITERATING on it.
  • YOUR IOSP BECOMES THE PROMPT — the written IOSP spec from Week 2 is exactly what you hand to lovable. A clear spec makes a clear app.

The free plan gives about 5 "build credits" per day (they don't roll over — roughly 30 a month). Every message you send the AI uses a credit. That's PLENTY if you plan first — but you can burn a whole day's credits on vague prompts. The golden rule: write your full plan on paper BEFORE you touch lovable.

Two ways to run this within free-tier limits: (1) Small groups of 2–3 share one build — great for collaboration and for stretching credits. (2) Teacher-driven — students write specs, and you build the class's chosen example live on the projector to model the process, then students build their own if accounts and time allow. Pick whichever fits your class; the shared/group model works especially well inside the free-tier budget.

Setup Checklist

  • Confirm every student's lovable.dev account works (they have approved accounts)
  • Confirm students can reach lovable.dev on the school network
  • Decide the model: individual vs. small-group vs. teacher-driven
  • Hand out the Project Guide
  • Set the norm loud and clear: planning happens on paper first

Define & Plan

DPTR + IOSP → a written spec (all on paper)

"This is the most important phase — and none of it uses lovable yet. A clear plan is what makes the build work on the first try."

Define (DPTR)

"Pick a real problem in YOUR life, family, school, or community — something you actually want fixed. In your Project Guide, answer: What's the problem? Who does it affect (this could be you)? How will you know the app helped?"

Steer students toward simple, real "information" apps a beginner AI build handles well — a study-reminder app, a chore/allowance tracker for their family, a water-intake tracker, a flashcard quizzer for a class they're taking, a simple club sign-up page, or a "what should I cook tonight?" picker. Warn away from anything needing logins to other services, payments, or lots of data.

Prepare (IOSP Spec)

"Now translate your idea into IOSP. What are the INPUTS (what the user types, taps, or chooses)? The OUTPUTS (what the app shows back)? What does it STORE? And the PROCESSING — remember the four kinds: if/then, comparing, finding a match, counting. This IOSP spec is the heart of your plan."

  • INPUT — what the user types, taps, or chooses.
  • OUTPUT — what the app shows back to the user.
  • STORAGE — what the app needs to remember between uses.
  • PROCESSING — the thinking work: if/then, comparing, finding a match, counting.

Write the Build Prompt

"Here's the payoff: your IOSP spec becomes the actual prompt you paste into lovable. Write it out fully, in order — Input, Output, Storage, Processing — plus a note on how it should look. Then it's ready to send as one strong first prompt."

  • EXAMPLE FIRST PROMPT — "Build a simple water-tracker web app. INPUT: a button to add a glass of water and a daily goal the user sets. OUTPUT: how many glasses today and a progress bar toward the goal. STORAGE: remember today's count. PROCESSING: compare the count to the goal and show an encouraging message when the goal is reached. Keep it colorful and simple for a middle-school user."

Credit-thrift: one strong first prompt that describes the whole app beats ten tiny ones. Plan the whole spec, send it as one clear prompt, THEN refine.

Every student/group has a written problem definition, an IOSP spec, and a drafted first build prompt — before opening lovable.

Build & Iterate

Spend credits wisely; refine in small loops

"Now open lovable and paste your first prompt. Watch it build. Then look carefully at what you got and improve it in small, specific steps."

Facilitating the Build

  • Circulate constantly — the build phase moves fast
  • Make sure students READ what the AI built before prompting again
  • Teach ONE change per prompt ("make the button bigger and blue," not five things at once)
  • If something breaks, lovable often offers a Fix option — have them use it
  • Remind them to save/keep the project so it isn't lost

Good Iteration Prompts

  • RENAME — "Change the title to 'My Water Tracker'."
  • ADD ONE THING — "Add a reset button for a new day."
  • TWEAK LOOK — "Make the progress bar green when I hit my goal."

Small, specific, one idea each. That's how you keep control of the build and your credits.

If a group is out of credits for the day, that's fine — they switch to PLANNING their next round of changes on paper so tomorrow's credits are spent instantly and well. Running out is a planning lesson, not a failure.

The goal is a WORKING app that solves the problem — not a perfect one. "Done and shared" beats "fancy and unfinished."

Publish

"When your app works, use lovable's Publish button to get a shareable web link. Copy that link into your Project Guide — that's your real, on-the-internet app."

Share & Reflect

Showcase, share with real people, reflect

"You built something real. Now show it, share it with the people who have the problem, and think about what you learned."

Showcase

"Gallery walk or short demos — each student or group shows their app, states the problem it solves, and walks through its IOSP: what goes In, what comes Out, what it Stores, and how it Processes."

  • Keep demos short — 1–2 minutes each
  • Ask each presenter to name the one processing type their app leans on most
  • Have the audience note one thing they'd want to build next

Extend Beyond the Classroom

"Share your app's link with the actual people who have this problem — your family, a friend, a teammate, a club. Ask them to try it and tell you one thing that helped and one thing to improve."

Reflect

  • Why does the problem you chose matter to you?
  • What did you have to make clear for the AI to build it right?
  • What would you build next?

"In one sentence: what problem does your app solve, and who is it for?"

Additional Notes for the Teacher

Managing the Free Tier (read this first)

  • About 5 build credits per day, no rollover, roughly 30 per month
  • Plan the full spec on paper first — every AI prompt costs a credit
  • One strong prompt beats many vague ones
  • Groups sharing a single build stretches credits and boosts collaboration
  • A day without credits is a planning day, not a lost day

Good Beginner App Ideas

  • Reminder and tracker apps (study, water, chores/allowance)
  • Flashcard quizzers for a class they're taking
  • Simple pickers/deciders ("what should I cook tonight?")
  • Club or event sign-up pages
  • A personal links or hobby page
  • Avoid payments, external logins, or heavy data

Differentiation

  • Struggling: Provide a fill-in-the-blank IOSP spec + a ready first-prompt template; let them personalize a provided example app rather than start from scratch
  • Advanced: Add a second feature, use more of the four processing types, design for a specific real audience, and fold that audience's feedback into a second iteration

If You're Running Behind

  • The Define & Plan phase is the priority — a great plan is most of the learning even if the build is short
  • The build can be a single strong prompt + publish
  • Sharing and reflection can be assigned as homework

Safety & Good Practice

  • Students should not put real personal info (full names, addresses, phone numbers) into their apps
  • Keep test data made-up
  • Review apps before sharing them widely
