The Problem Solving Process

Week 1 — Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect: the process behind every solution

At a Glance

Driving Question
What common steps can we use to solve many different kinds of problems?
Big Idea
Good problem solvers follow a repeatable process — Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect (DPTR).
Schedule
Day 1 Learn (45 min) · Day 2 Explore (90 min) · Day 3 Create (90 min)
Week Deliverable
Completed Problem Solving Process Notes (DPTR reflection + purpose of each step)

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • Name and describe the four steps of the problem solving process (Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect)
  • Apply the DPTR process to solve a non-computer problem
  • Explain the purpose of each step and why the process helps
  • Recognize that the same process is used in computer science

Day 1 — Learn: The Problem Solving Process

Discovering the four steps every good problem solver uses

Time Activity
0–7 min Hook — journal prompt
7–20 min Mini-lesson — the DPTR cycle
20–30 min Watch Code.org "Problem Solving Process with Zipline" video + discuss
30–40 min Quick practice — map your own process
40–45 min Exit ticket

Hook (7 min)

"Think about a problem you're really good at solving. Could be anything — winning a video game level, making a recipe, fixing an argument with a friend. In your journal, write down: what's a problem you're good at solving, and what steps do you take?"

Give students 3 quiet minutes to write, then have 3–4 share aloud. Accept ALL answers — the point is that everyone already solves problems with steps, even if they've never named them.

Mini-lesson: The DPTR Cycle (13 min)

"It turns out that great problem solvers — engineers, chefs, athletes, coders — all use the same basic process, even when they're solving totally different problems. We call it DPTR: Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect. Let's walk through what each step means."

Present each step with its guiding questions:

  • DEFINE — What problem are you trying to solve? What are your constraints? What does success look like?
  • PREPARE — Brainstorm and research possible solutions. Compare pros and cons. Make a plan.
  • TRY — Put your plan into action.
  • REFLECT — How do your results compare to your goals? What can you learn or do better next time? What new problems did you discover?

DPTR is a CYCLE, not a straight line — Reflect often loops right back to Define. Draw the circle on the board: Define → Prepare → Try → Reflect → back to Define. Trace the loop with your finger so students see it never really "ends."

Video: Problem Solving with Zipline (10 min)

"Let's see this in the real world. We'll watch how an engineer at Zipline — a company that delivers medical supplies by drone — uses this exact process."

  • Play the video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nZuVchNli5c
  • Then run the discussion questions below

After the video, discuss:

  1. "Which step did Ryan spend the most time on? Why?"
  2. "Why do you think defining the problem matters so much when lives are on the line?"
  3. "Where did you see 'Reflect' lead back to 'Define'?"

Quick Practice: Map Your Own Process (10 min)

"Go back to the problem you journaled about at the start of class. Label your own steps with D, P, T, or R. Which steps did you skip? Most people skip Reflect!"

Have a few students share which step they skipped. This makes the point that a process only works when you use ALL of it — especially the step people rush past.

"In one sentence each, describe what happens in Define, Prepare, Try, and Reflect."

Every student can name all four steps and has mapped their own problem to the DPTR cycle.

Day 2 — Explore: Practicing the Process

Using DPTR to solve a tricky seating puzzle

Time Activity
0–5 min Review DPTR
5–15 min Warm-up — Word Search
15–20 min Introduce the Birthday Guests challenge
20–55 min Hands-on lab — solve Birthday Guests in teams
55–75 min Guided debrief — map each move to DPTR
75–90 min Reflection — fill the DPTR table

Review (5 min)

"Quick warm-up for our brains: without looking at your notes, what are the four steps of the problem solving process? Call them out."

Cold-call four different students, one step each. If anyone's stuck, let a classmate help. Keep it fast and low-pressure — this is a recall check, not a quiz.

Warm-up: Word Search (10 min)

"Warm up your problem-solving brain with a word search on your worksheet. Find all 8 words. As you search, notice HOW you search — that's a process too."

The 8 words are: DEFINE, PREPARE, TRY, REFLECT, PROBLEM, SOLVE, COMPUTER, SCIENCE. Tell students to notice their own strategy (scanning rows first? looking for capital letters? starting with long words?) — you'll connect that to "Prepare" later.

Introduce Birthday Guests (5 min)

"Here's a real puzzle. 15 guests at a birthday, 3 tables that seat 5 each. Some friends want to sit together. Some people are in a fight and must be kept apart. Your job: find a seating arrangement that makes everyone as happy as possible. This is on your worksheet."

The constraints (also on the student worksheet):

Close Friends — Keep Together

  • Aysha & Damien
  • Max & Isaias
  • Nazek & Laila
  • Owen & Genaro
  • Ben & Jessica
  • Genaro & Eric

In a Fight — Keep Apart

  • Aysha & Genaro
  • Ben & Hannah
  • Fan & Max
  • Damien & Laila
  • Isaias & Owen
  • Kyla & Jessica

Hands-on Lab (35 min)

Facilitation during lab time:

  • Circulate; don't give answers. Ask "What have you defined? What's your plan?"
  • If a team is stuck, prompt them to write the constraints as a list first (that's Prepare)
  • Remind teams there may be more than one good answer — "best possible," not "perfect"
  • Watch for teams that just guess randomly (no Prepare) — point them back to making a plan

Some students find this frustrating. That's the point — reflect the frustration back as evidence they're in the "Try" step, where plans meet reality. Encourage iterating: Try → Reflect → Prepare again.

Guided Debrief (20 min)

"Let's walk through the process together as a class. How did you DEFINE this problem? The goal was maximum happiness; the constraints were table size plus the friendships and fights. How did you PREPARE? Maybe you listed the constraints, or paired up the close friends first. How did you TRY? You placed people and adjusted. How did you REFLECT? You counted the conflicts and rearranged."

As students name each step, jot it on the board under D / P / T / R. Seeing their own moves sorted into the four buckets is the "aha" — they used the process even if they didn't call it that.

One valid arrangement. There are several correct answers — the goal is that ALL six "keep together" and ALL six "keep apart" rules hold. This solution satisfies every constraint. Verify a student solution the same way: check each pair, not the whole table at once.

Table 1: Nazek, Laila, Max, Isaias, Kyla

Together met: Nazek+Laila ✓, Max+Isaias ✓. Apart met: Damien/Laila (Damien elsewhere) ✓, Fan/Max (Fan elsewhere) ✓, Isaias/Owen (Owen elsewhere) ✓, Kyla/Jessica (Jessica elsewhere) ✓.

Table 2: Owen, Genaro, Eric, Hannah, Fan

Together met: Owen+Genaro ✓, Genaro+Eric ✓. Apart met: Aysha/Genaro (Aysha elsewhere) ✓, Isaias/Owen (Isaias elsewhere) ✓, Fan/Max (Max elsewhere) ✓, Ben/Hannah (Ben elsewhere) ✓.

Table 3: Ben, Jessica, Aysha, Damien, Carla

Together met: Ben+Jessica ✓, Aysha+Damien ✓. Apart met: Ben/Hannah (Hannah elsewhere) ✓, Aysha/Genaro (Genaro elsewhere) ✓, Kyla/Jessica (Kyla elsewhere) ✓, Damien/Laila (Laila elsewhere) ✓.

Full check: all 15 guests seated, tables of 5/5/5. All 6 "together" pairs share a table; all 6 "apart" pairs are split across tables. The tricky part students hit: Ben must sit with Jessica but away from Hannah, and Jessica must stay away from Kyla — so Hannah and Kyla have to land at different tables from Ben/Jessica. Putting Hannah at Table 2 and Kyla at Table 1 resolves it.

Reflection (15 min)

"On your worksheet, fill in the Problem Solving Process Notes table for both the Word Search and Birthday Guests — what did Define, Prepare, Try, and Reflect look like for each activity?"

Every team produced a seating arrangement and can explain how they used at least 3 of the 4 DPTR steps.

Day 3 — Create: Redesign Your Classroom

Applying the full process to a real design challenge

Time Activity
0–5 min Launch the challenge
5–15 min DEFINE — develop goals (group)
15–40 min PREPARE + TRY — design a layout individually, then compare
40–60 min Share layouts & get peer feedback
60–75 min REFLECT — improve and finalize as a group
75–90 min Complete Problem Solving Process Notes + quick shares

Launch (5 min)

"Today you use the whole process on a real problem: redesign this classroom. You have 'one school day' to do it. Make the best classroom you can — but you have to justify every choice with a goal."

Define — Develop Goals (10 min)

"In your group, agree on your goals. What makes a good classroom layout? Everyone can see the teacher? Easy to move around? Write your goals on the worksheet — these are your success criteria."

Push groups to write 3–5 concrete goals, not just "make it nice." Good goals are testable, like "every seat can see the board" — those become the criteria they'll judge designs against later.

Prepare + Try — Design a Layout (25 min)

"Now EACH person designs their own layout that meets the goals. Draw it. For each goal, note how your design meets it."

Share Layouts & Peer Feedback (20 min)

"Share your layout with your group. Explain WHY it's the best given your goals. Group members: give 'glow and grow' — one strength, one suggestion."

Model glow-and-grow once before groups start. Feedback must be specific ("the reading corner is far from the door") not vague ("it's good"). Specific feedback is the only kind that helps them improve.

Reflect — Improve & Finalize (15 min)

"Using your group's feedback, agree on ONE combined design. Then finish your Problem Solving Process Notes."

Week 1 Deliverable Rubric

For the Problem Solving Process Notes. Use it to give quick, consistent feedback.

Criteria Beginning Proficient Exemplary
Identifies all 4 DPTR steps Names 1–2 steps Names all 4 steps correctly All 4 correct, in order, in own words
Explains purpose of each step Little or no explanation Explains most steps' purpose Clear purpose for every step
Gives examples from activities No specific examples An example for some steps Specific examples across activities
Reflects on improving next time No reflection Names one improvement Thoughtful, specific improvements

Every group submitted a finalized classroom design and each student completed their Problem Solving Process Notes.

Additional Notes for the Teacher

Differentiation

  • Struggling: Provide DPTR hint cards; give sentence starters for reflection ("In Define, I figured out ___"); let them do Birthday Guests with only 2 tables and 10 guests
  • Advanced: Add a 4th constraint to Birthday Guests (e.g., one more fight); ask them to find a SECOND valid arrangement; challenge: "Where could a computer do this faster than you?"

Materials Needed

  • Student worksheet (printed or digital)
  • Journals
  • Board or projector for the video
  • Pencils and markers for layouts

If you're running behind

  • The Word Search can be assigned as homework; the Birthday Guests debrief is the priority on Day 2
  • The Day 3 redesign peer-feedback round can be shortened to 10 min
