Lesson 1

What Is a Problem?

A "problem" isn't always something bad. In problem solving, a problem is any situation where you want to get from where you are now to somewhere better. There's a starting point, a goal, and a gap in between that you need to cross.

That means you're already solving problems all the time — you just might not call them that:

  • Beating a tricky level in a video game
  • Planning a birthday party your friends will love
  • Figuring out why your code (or your bike) won't work

Big or small, silly or serious — they all share the same shape. And that's why one process can help you solve almost any of them.

Lesson 2

The Four Steps (DPTR)

Great problem solvers — engineers, chefs, athletes, coders — tend to follow the same four steps, even when their problems look nothing alike. We call it DPTR: Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect.

Define — understand the problem

  • What am I actually trying to solve?
  • What are my constraints (my rules and limits)?
  • What does success look like?

Prepare — get ready with a plan

  • Brainstorm as many options as I can.
  • Compare the pros and cons of each.
  • Choose one and make a plan.

Try — put the plan into action

  • Actually do it — test my plan in the real world.

Reflect — learn from what happened

  • Did it work? How do my results compare to my goal?
  • What would I do better next time?
  • What new problems appeared?

DPTR is a cycle, not a straight line. When you Reflect, you almost always loop right back to Define with a sharper idea of the problem. Define → Prepare → Try → Reflect → back to Define. It never really "ends" — it just gets better each loop.

Challenge

Birthday Guests

Time to use DPTR on a real puzzle. You're planning a party for 15 guests seated at 3 tables of 5. Some guests must sit together; some must sit apart. Your job: find a seating that satisfies every rule.

The 15 Guests Aysha, Damien, Max, Isaias, Nazek, Laila, Owen, Genaro, Eric, Ben, Jessica, Hannah, Fan, Kyla, Carla

Must Sit Together

  • Aysha & Damien
  • Max & Isaias
  • Nazek & Laila
  • Owen & Genaro
  • Ben & Jessica
  • Genaro & Eric

Must Sit Apart

  • Aysha & Genaro
  • Ben & Hannah
  • Fan & Max
  • Damien & Laila
  • Isaias & Owen
  • Kyla & Jessica

Tip: Start by Defining the rules, then Prepare by grouping the "together" pairs first. Write your attempt below — each table holds exactly 5 guests.

Check your thinking

Here's one valid solution:

Table 1

Nazek, Laila, Max, Isaias, Kyla

Table 2

Owen, Genaro, Eric, Hannah, Fan

Table 3

Ben, Jessica, Aysha, Damien, Carla

Check it: all six "together" pairs share a table, and all six "apart" pairs are split up. The tricky part is that both Hannah and Kyla have to sit away from Ben & Jessica's table — that's what forces the rest of the arrangement into place.

There's more than one right answer — if all your rules are satisfied, you solved it!

Wrap-Up

The Big Idea

One process works for every kind of problem. Whether you're planning a party, seating party guests, or building a drone delivery system, the same four steps — Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect — help you cross the gap from where you are to where you want to be. And because it's a cycle, you get a little better every time you loop through it.
