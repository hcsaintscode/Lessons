The Problem Solving Process

Week 1 — Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect: a repeatable engine for solving hard problems

At a Glance

Driving Question
What repeatable process lets us solve problems well enough that we could even hand them to a computer?
Big Idea
A disciplined problem-solving process (Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect) turns messy problems into ones we can reason about — and eventually automate.
Schedule
Day 1 Learn (45 min) · Day 2 Explore (90 min) · Day 3 Create (90 min)
Week Deliverable
Problem Solving Process Notes with written justifications + a short analysis of where a computer could automate each problem

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • Describe the four steps of the problem solving process and apply them deliberately
  • Model a real problem in terms of constraints, trade-offs, and success criteria
  • Distinguish a "satisfiable" solution (meets all constraints) from an "optimal" one
  • Identify which parts of a problem could be automated by a computer, and explain how

Day 1 — Learn: The Problem Solving Process

A disciplined process for problems worth solving

Time Activity
0–6 min Hook — journal
6–20 min Mini-lesson — DPTR + the vocabulary of problems (constraints, trade-offs, success criteria)
20–30 min Zipline video + analysis
30–40 min Apply — model a problem formally
40–45 min Exit ticket

Hook (6 min)

"Think of a problem you solve well — a game, a sport, coding, cooking, an argument. In your journal: write the problem, then break down the exact steps you take. Be specific enough that someone else could follow your steps."

The 'specific enough someone else could follow it' framing matters — it's the seed of ALGORITHMIC thinking. Flag that connection now; you'll return to it all semester.

Mini-lesson: DPTR + the Vocabulary of Problems (14 min)

"Experts across every field — engineers, coders, designers — lean on the same underlying process even when the problems look nothing alike. We call it DPTR: Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect. Today we'll also pick up the precise vocabulary that lets us describe any problem clearly."

Present each step with its guiding questions:

  • DEFINE — What exactly is the problem? What are the constraints? What does success look like (your success criteria)?
  • PREPARE — Brainstorm approaches, compare their trade-offs, and commit to a plan.
  • TRY — Execute the plan.
  • REFLECT — Compare results against your success criteria, iterate to improve, and surface the new problems your solution created.

Now introduce the vocabulary students will use all week — write these on the board and have students copy them:

Constraints = the rules/limits a solution must respect.
Trade-offs = when improving one thing costs you another.
Success criteria = how you'll measure whether you solved it.
Satisfiable vs optimal = "meets all the rules" vs "the best possible."

REFLECT is what makes DPTR a cycle: it loops back to Define, so problem solving is iteration, not a one-shot line. Draw the loop and trace it.

Zipline Video + Analysis (10 min)

"Watch how an engineer at Zipline applies this exact process to a life-or-death logistics problem."

  • Play the video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nZuVchNli5c
  • Then run the analysis questions below

After the video, discuss:

  1. "What were Zipline's constraints?"
  2. "What trade-offs did the engineer weigh?"
  3. "What were their success criteria?"
  4. "Where did iteration (Reflect → Define) show up?"

Apply: Model a Problem Formally (10 min)

"Take your journal problem and rewrite it formally: list its constraints, its trade-offs, and its success criteria. Then answer: could a computer solve this? What would it need as input?"

This is the day's key move — reframing an everyday problem in formal terms. Circulate and push for specificity. Vague criteria ("make it good") become testable ones ("finishes in under 5 minutes").

"Define constraints, trade-offs, and success criteria in your own words, and give one example of each from your problem."

Every student modeled a problem in terms of constraints, trade-offs, and success criteria, and speculated about automation.

Day 2 — Explore: Constraint Problems

Solving — and analyzing — a constraint satisfaction problem

Time Activity
0–5 min Review vocabulary
5–12 min Warm-up — Word Search (framed as a search algorithm)
12–20 min Introduce Birthday Guests as a constraint satisfaction problem
20–50 min Solve individually, then compare in pairs
50–70 min Debrief — model the problem formally + "how would a computer solve this?"
70–90 min Reflection + written justification

Review (5 min)

"Quick recall, no notes: define constraints, trade-offs, and success criteria. And what's the difference between a satisfiable solution and an optimal one?"

Cold-call four students, one term each. Keep it fast — this is a recall check that primes the vocabulary you'll use throughout the lab.

Warm-up: Word Search as a Search Algorithm (7 min)

"Word search on your worksheet — find all 8 words. But this time, pay attention to your SEARCH STRATEGY. Are you scanning row by row? Looking for rare letters? That strategy is an algorithm — a step-by-step method. Write down the strategy you used."

Connect explicitly: a word search is a SEARCH problem, exactly the kind computers are great at. Ask "how would you tell a computer to find a word in this grid?" Students should start describing inputs (the grid, the target word) and a rule for checking each position — that's the seed of an algorithm.

Introduce Birthday Guests (8 min)

"Here's a classic constraint satisfaction problem. 15 guests, 3 tables of 5. Some pairs MUST sit together (a constraint). Some pairs MUST sit apart (a constraint). Find an arrangement that satisfies ALL constraints. This is exactly the kind of problem computers solve — scheduling, seating, logistics."

The constraints (also on the student worksheet):

Must Sit Together

  • Aysha & Damien
  • Max & Isaias
  • Nazek & Laila
  • Owen & Genaro
  • Ben & Jessica
  • Genaro & Eric

Must Sit Apart

  • Aysha & Genaro
  • Ben & Hannah
  • Fan & Max
  • Damien & Laila
  • Isaias & Owen
  • Kyla & Jessica

Solve Individually, Then Compare (30 min)

Facilitation during work time:

  • Students work SOLO first (10 min) before pairing — independent struggle is the point
  • Push them to Prepare systematically: list constraints, chain the "together" pairs (Owen–Genaro–Eric form a trio; that trio must be one table)
  • Ask "Is your solution satisfiable? Is it optimal? What would 'optimal' even mean here?"
  • For fast finishers: "How many valid arrangements exist? How would you know?"

Resist giving the answer. If a pair is stuck, ask a Socratic question ("Which guest has the most constraints? Start there.") Genaro is the most-constrained (in two 'together' pairs and one 'apart') — a great starting heuristic to surface.

Debrief: Model It Formally (20 min)

"Let's model this problem formally as a class. The VARIABLES are the guests. The DOMAIN of each variable is which table they sit at — 1, 2, or 3. The CONSTRAINTS are the rules: a 'together' pair means same table; an 'apart' pair means different tables. This is a CONSTRAINT SATISFACTION PROBLEM. A computer could solve it by trying combinations and checking the constraints — that's called search. Later this year you'll write code that does exactly this."

Write the model on the board: variables → domains → constraints. Naming the structure is what separates HS from earlier grades — students should be able to reproduce this framing for a new problem.

One verified arrangement. Several valid answers exist — the goal is that ALL six "together" and ALL six "apart" constraints hold. Verify a student solution the same way: check each pair, not the whole table at once.

Table 1: Nazek, Laila, Max, Isaias, Kyla

Together met: Nazek+Laila ✓, Max+Isaias ✓. Apart met: Damien/Laila (Damien elsewhere) ✓, Fan/Max (Fan elsewhere) ✓, Isaias/Owen (Owen elsewhere) ✓, Kyla/Jessica (Jessica elsewhere) ✓.

Table 2: Owen, Genaro, Eric, Hannah, Fan

Together met: Owen+Genaro ✓, Genaro+Eric ✓. Apart met: Aysha/Genaro (Aysha elsewhere) ✓, Isaias/Owen (Isaias elsewhere) ✓, Fan/Max (Max elsewhere) ✓, Ben/Hannah (Ben elsewhere) ✓.

Table 3: Ben, Jessica, Aysha, Damien, Carla

Together met: Ben+Jessica ✓, Aysha+Damien ✓. Apart met: Ben/Hannah (Hannah elsewhere) ✓, Aysha/Genaro (Genaro elsewhere) ✓, Kyla/Jessica (Kyla elsewhere) ✓, Damien/Laila (Laila elsewhere) ✓.

Key insight: Owen–Genaro–Eric is a forced trio (chained "together" pairs), so they must share a table. Genaro is the most-constrained guest (two "together" pairs plus an "apart"), so it's the best place to start. Hannah and Kyla must avoid the Ben/Jessica table (Ben/Hannah apart, Kyla/Jessica apart), so they land at Tables 2 and 1 respectively.

Satisfiable vs optimal: This problem is satisfiable with several valid arrangements; there's no single "optimal" unless we define an objective (e.g., maximize friend-adjacencies) — a good discussion point.

Reflection + Written Justification (20 min)

"On your worksheet, complete the Process Notes for Word Search and Birthday Guests, and write a short justification: which DPTR steps did you use, and where could a computer take over?"

Every student produced a satisfiable arrangement and can describe the problem formally (variables, domains, constraints) + name where a computer could automate it.

Day 3 — Create: Redesign Your Classroom

Applying the full process to an open-ended design problem

Time Activity
0–5 min Launch + connect to design problems
5–18 min DEFINE — success criteria & constraints (individual, then group)
18–45 min PREPARE + TRY — design competing layouts, evaluate against criteria
45–62 min Peer critique (structured)
62–78 min REFLECT — iterate to a final design + trade-off analysis
78–90 min Complete Process Notes + automation analysis

Launch (5 min)

"Now an open-ended DESIGN problem — no single right answer. Redesign this classroom. Unlike Birthday Guests, the constraints are fuzzy and you must DEFINE your own success criteria. That's what makes real design hard."

Define — Success Criteria & Constraints (13 min)

"Individually, write 4–6 success criteria and the constraints (fixed things you can't change — room size, number of desks, the door). Then agree on a shared set as a group."

Distinguish constraints (can't change: outlets, door, room dimensions) from criteria (goals you're optimizing: sightlines, flow, collaboration). HS students should separate these cleanly — a constraint is a hard limit, a criterion is something you're trying to maximize.

Prepare + Try — Competing Layouts (27 min)

"Each person designs a layout. For each success criterion, rate how well your design meets it (1–5). This is a trade-off analysis — you can't maximize everything."

Peer Critique (17 min)

"Structured critique: each designer presents; reviewers name one criterion the design serves well and one trade-off it makes. Reference the criteria, not opinions."

Model one critique before groups start. "Serves sightlines well but trades away group-work space" is useful; "I like it" is not. Anchoring feedback to the agreed criteria keeps it objective.

Reflect — Iterate + Trade-off Analysis (16 min)

"As a group, synthesize the best design. Write a short trade-off analysis: what did you optimize for, and what did you sacrifice?"

Additional Notes for the Teacher

Differentiation

  • Support: Provide a constraint-listing template; let them solve Birthday Guests with the trio (Owen/Genaro/Eric) pre-placed at a table
  • Challenge: Ask students to (a) count the number of valid arrangements and justify, (b) define an objective function that makes one arrangement 'optimal', or (c) write pseudocode for how a computer would search for a solution

Connecting to the Semester Arc

  • This week plants Programming (the automation thread), AI/ML (search & optimization), and the engineering mindset. Keep asking "how would a computer do this?"

Materials Needed

  • Student worksheet, journals, projector for the video, grid paper or a drawing tool for layouts

If you're running behind

  • Word Search is optional/homework; prioritize the Birthday Guests formal debrief
  • The Day 3 trade-off analysis is the key HS deliverable — protect time for it
