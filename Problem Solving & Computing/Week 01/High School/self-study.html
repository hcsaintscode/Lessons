Lesson 1

What Makes a Problem Solvable?

Strip away the surface details and every problem has the same skeleton: a start state (where things are now), a goal state (where you want them to be), and a set of constraints that govern the space in between. Solving the problem means finding a path from start to goal without violating any constraint.

This framing is not just tidy — it's powerful. The moment you can describe a problem this precisely, you can begin to reason about whether a computer could solve it, because a machine needs exactly these three things stated unambiguously. Clearly defining a problem is the first step toward being able to automate it.

  • Route planning — start: your location; goal: the destination; constraints: roads, traffic, distance, time.
  • Scheduling — start: a set of tasks and people; goal: everything assigned; constraints: availability, no double-booking, deadlines.
  • Debugging — start: broken behavior; goal: correct behavior; constraints: the existing code, the inputs, the environment.

Notice the pattern: each one is just states plus constraints. If you can't yet name the start, goal, and constraints, you haven't finished defining the problem.

Lesson 2

The Four Steps (DPTR)

Skilled problem solvers — engineers, researchers, designers, developers — converge on the same four-step discipline even across wildly different domains. We call it DPTR: Define, Prepare, Try, Reflect. Treat each step as a distinct mode of thinking, not a box to rush through.

Define — frame the problem precisely

  • What exactly is the problem? State the start state and goal state.
  • What are the constraints — the hard rules and limits I cannot break?
  • What are my success criteria? How will I measure whether I've solved it?

Prepare — weigh options and commit to a plan

  • Generate multiple candidate approaches, not just the first one.
  • Evaluate the trade-offs of each — what does one option cost you that another saves?
  • Choose deliberately and lay out a plan you can execute and test.

Try — run the plan as an experiment

  • Execute the plan, treating it as a test of your hypothesis rather than a final answer.
  • Capture what actually happens — the evidence you'll need to reflect on.

Reflect — measure, iterate, and surface new problems

  • Measure the result against your success criteria. Did it meet them, and by how much?
  • Iterate: what would you change, and what did the attempt teach you about the problem?
  • What new problems or sub-problems did this attempt reveal?

DPTR is a cycle, not a straight line. Reflection almost always loops back to Define with a sharper, better-instrumented understanding of the problem. Define → Prepare → Try → Reflect → back to Define. The point isn't to finish the loop once — it's that each pass through the loop makes the next one smarter. This is iteration, and it's how hard problems actually get solved.

Lesson 3

The Language of Problems

To reason about problems rigorously — and to hand them to a computer later — you need shared, precise vocabulary. These four terms will recur throughout the course.

Constraints — the rules you cannot break

  • Conditions any valid solution must satisfy. In seating: "these two people must sit together."
  • Constraints shrink the space of possible solutions to only the legal ones.

Trade-offs — what you give up to gain something

  • Two good options rarely dominate each other on every axis. Faster often costs more; simpler often does less.
  • Naming trade-offs turns a vague "which is better?" into a decision you can defend.

Success criteria — how you'll know it worked

  • The measurable conditions that define "solved." Without them, you can't tell progress from motion.
  • Good criteria are testable: "every guest seated, no rule violated" beats "seems fine."

Satisfiable vs. Optimal — legal versus best

  • Satisfiable: a solution that breaks no constraints. There may be many.
  • Optimal: the best satisfiable solution according to some objective (cheapest, fastest, fewest tables). "Best" is meaningless until you define the objective you're optimizing.

Keep these four in mind for the video and the challenge ahead — you'll be asked to use them by name.

Challenge

Birthday Guests — Constraint Satisfaction

Here's a genuine constraint satisfaction problem. You're seating 15 guests at 3 tables of 5. Some guests must sit together; some must sit apart. Your job: find a seating that satisfies every constraint. This is exactly the kind of problem computers are asked to solve at scale.

The 15 Guests Aysha, Damien, Max, Isaias, Nazek, Laila, Owen, Genaro, Eric, Ben, Jessica, Hannah, Fan, Kyla, Carla

Must Sit Together

  • Aysha & Damien
  • Max & Isaias
  • Nazek & Laila
  • Owen & Genaro
  • Ben & Jessica
  • Genaro & Eric

Must Sit Apart

  • Aysha & Genaro
  • Ben & Hannah
  • Fan & Max
  • Damien & Laila
  • Isaias & Owen
  • Kyla & Jessica

Don't jump straight to guessing. Model it first — then search deliberately.

Now place all 15 — each table holds exactly 5 guests.

Check your thinking

Here's one valid solution:

Table 1

Nazek, Laila, Max, Isaias, Kyla

Table 2

Owen, Genaro, Eric, Hannah, Fan

Table 3

Ben, Jessica, Aysha, Damien, Carla

Verify it: all six "together" pairs share a table, and all six "apart" pairs are separated. The key structural insight is that Owen–Genaro–Eric form a forced trio — the "together" constraints chain (Owen&Genaro, then Genaro&Eric), so all three must land at one table. That makes Genaro the most-constrained guest: he's locked to two others and also barred from sitting with Aysha, so pinning his table down first collapses most of the search.

From there, Hannah and Kyla must both avoid Ben & Jessica's table (Ben–Hannah apart, Kyla–Jessica apart), which pushes them onto the other two tables and settles the rest.

This problem is satisfiable, and there is more than one valid solution — if your seating breaks no constraints, you solved it. Note that "optimal" would only be meaningful once you define an objective to optimize (for example, minimizing empty seats or balancing table sizes); satisfiability alone doesn't rank one valid answer above another.

Reflect

Automation Analysis

You just solved the seating puzzle as a human. Now think like a computer scientist: how would you hand this exact problem to a machine? A computer can't "just see" a good seating — it needs the problem represented formally and a procedure to follow.

This is the bridge to what's ahead. In the Programming phase you'll turn representations and algorithms like these into working code, and in the AI/ML phase you'll see machines search enormous constraint spaces far faster than any human could by hand. Learning to model precisely now is what makes all of that possible.

Wrap-Up

The Big Idea

Define a problem precisely enough and it becomes something you can automate. Start state, goal state, constraints, success criteria — once a problem is stated in that language, it stops being a vague situation and becomes a well-posed task a machine can be given. DPTR is the discipline that gets you there, and because it's a cycle, every loop sharpens the definition further.
